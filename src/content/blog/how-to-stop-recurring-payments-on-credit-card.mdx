Canceling a subscription should take one click. But what happens when the gym claims you never sent certified mail, the software provider has removed their cancel button, or a company keeps hitting your debit card weeks after you closed your account?

Under federal banking regulations (including the **Electronic Fund Transfer Act** and Regulation E), **you have legal rights to halt automated recurring charges**.

Here is the exact legal and banking playbook to permanently stop unwanted recurring credit and debit card payments.

---

## Step 1: Revoke Authorization in Writing (The Paper Trail)

Banks require proof that you officially attempted to cancel before they will process a chargeback or merchant block.

1. **Send an email or message to the company:**  
   *"I am revoking authorization for all recurring debits and subscription fees associated with account [Your Account ID / Email]. Confirm cancellation and cease all future billing immediately."*
2. **Screenshot the confirmation page, email timestamp, or support ticket number.**
3. **Download your contract or terms of service:** Check the required cancellation notice window (e.g., 30-day notice).

If the merchant bills you again after receiving your revocation notice, they are committing an **unauthorized transaction**, giving your bank grounds for an immediate reversal.

---

## Step 2: Issue a Stop Payment Order with Your Bank

If a company is set up as an automatic ACH debit on your checking account or debit card, you can instruct your financial institution to stop the transfer.

- **Chase Bank:** Call 1-800-935-9935 or log into Chase Online → Customer Center → Stop Payment on a Debit/ACH.
- **Bank of America:** Call 1-800-432-1000 or use Mobile Banking → Pay & Transfer → Stop Payment.
- **Wells Fargo:** Call 1-800-869-3557 or submit via Account Services → Stop Payment.
- **Capital One / Discover / Amex:** Log in online or call the number on the back of your card to request a recurring merchant block.

> **Important Note:** A Stop Payment order typically costs between $15 to $30 (though many credit unions and premium cards waive this). Provide the exact company billing descriptor (e.g., *"GYM*PLANETFITNESS 800-555"*).

---

## Step 3: Beware the Credit Card "Account Updater" Loophole

A common frustration is ordering a new replacement credit card, only to find the unwanted subscription charge shows up on the new card the following week.

**Why does this happen?**  
Visa and Mastercard provide automated services called **VAU (Visa Account Updater)** and **ABU (Automatic Billing Updater)**. When your card number changes due to expiration or loss, credit bureaus automatically push your new card details to recurring merchants so payments don't fail.

### How to break the loop:
When you call your bank to replace a card due to stubborn merchant billing, explicitly state:  
**"Please opt me out of the Visa/Mastercard Automatic Billing Updater for this merchant, or block merchant tokens."**

---

## Step 4: When and How to Dispute the Charge (Chargeback)

If a merchant debited your account after you canceled and refused to refund you, file a chargeback:

| Timeframe | Action | Evidence Required |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **0–60 Days from Statement** | File formal dispute online via bank app or phone | Date of cancellation email, merchant refusal response |
| **Dispute Reason** | Select *"Canceled Recurring Transaction"* or *"Paid by other means / service not received"* | Bank statement showing repeated charge |
| **Bank Resolution** | Bank provides provisional credit within 5–10 business days | Merchant has 30–45 days to respond |

Under FTC guidelines, merchants must provide a simple cancellation mechanism ("click to cancel"). If they make cancellation intentionally deceptive or impossible (dark patterns), report them to the **Federal Trade Commission (ftc.gov/complaint)** and your state Attorney General.

## Preventing Stubborn Recurring Charges

To avoid ever fighting your bank again:
1. **Never use debit cards for online subscriptions:** Always use a credit card, which has far stronger zero-liability protections under the Fair Credit Billing Act.
